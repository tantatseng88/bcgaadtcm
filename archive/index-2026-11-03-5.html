Newsletter post 1: Singapore's AI build-out is now a balance-sheet story, and that raises the bar for value
SINGAPORE / CLOUD
Full post | 176 words | Thought-leadership voice
Reports this week say STT GDC is negotiating a S$3.1 billion syndicated loan, arranged by DBS, OCBC, UOB and Standard Chartered, only weeks after a KKR and Singtel-led consortium completed its S$6.6 billion acquisition of the operator. Bloomberg counts more than US$650 billion of AI-linked debt raised since early 2025.
For Singapore this is good news on capacity. Data centres, power and connectivity are the foundation every enterprise AI roadmap quietly assumes. But it also changes the conversation inside our organisations. When infrastructure is financed on multi-year terms against expected AI demand, the market is betting that enterprises will turn that capacity into productivity and revenue.
My view is that the constraint is shifting from "can we get compute?" to "can we show value per workload?" That puts pressure on what technology teams often leave to the end: unit economics of AI workloads, FinOps discipline, and value tracking a CFO would recognise.
Takeaway: before asking for more AI capacity, be ready to show the value each workload already returns, in numbers the finance team will sign.
Takeaway: Show measured value per AI workload before asking for more capacity.

Newsletter post 2: Public-sector AI is getting its first honest audit: claimed savings versus measured savings
OUTSIDE SINGAPORE
Full post | 196 words | Thought-leadership voice
This week's round-up of government AI news reads like a field test of transformation discipline. In the UK, the Budget leans on AI to justify a 20% cut in departmental administration budgets by 2029-30, citing around three hours a week saved by a teacher lesson-planning tool. The independent trial found about 49 minutes. In the same week a minister told Parliament that Capita had failed to deliver an AI-enabled pensions service, with around 1,800 complex cases open for over a year.
Meanwhile Rome reports its Julia 3.0 assistant now completes 30 services end to end in chat.
The contrast is instructive. Rome's claim is narrow, specific and measurable: a named service and a before-and-after time. The UK claims are broad, and the evidence trails the promise. Neither outcome is about the model. Both are about how the value case is framed, measured and governed.
My view is that every AI business case should carry two numbers side by side, the pilot or vendor estimate and the independently measured result, reviewed at each stage gate. Budgets should be cut only against the second number.
Takeaway: bank AI savings when they are measured, not when they are announced.
Takeaway: Bank AI savings when they are measured, not when they are announced.

Newsletter post 3: Data gravity is back: the next AI architecture decision is where the model runs
ENTERPRISE IT / CLOUD
Full post | 183 words | Thought-leadership voice
At Cloudera's EVOLVE26 event, the company framed its AI strategy in one line: take the models to where the data already lives, rather than shipping data out to external AI services. Its partnership with Mistral AI puts those models inside its data platform, and the reasons given were telling: regulations such as the EU's DORA and the US CLOUD Act, and the rising cost of public AI services as usage scales.
This matters in Singapore too. Deloitte's 2026 State of AI survey found 77% of Singapore leaders see data residency and in-country or in-region compute as strategic, and 57% worry about over-reliance on foreign-owned AI. For regulated sectors, and for logistics and manufacturing firms holding trade data across borders, the question is shifting from "which model is best?" to "where should inference run, and who controls the data path?"
My view: treat model placement as an enterprise architecture decision with three tests: data sensitivity, regulatory exposure, and cost at production volume rather than pilot volume.
Takeaway: design for a portfolio of models placed close to the data, with an exit route for each.
Takeaway: Place models close to the data, and keep an exit route for each.

Newsletter post 4: UBS's real AI metric is 608 use cases in production, not 12,700 agents
ENTERPRISE IT / GLOBAL
Full post | 198 words | Thought-leadership voice
UBS's third-quarter update included an unusual set of AI numbers: more than 12,700 personal AI agents in use across its workforce, 608 use cases live in production (up 81% year on year), and over 1,090 more in development. Few large enterprises publish a production count at all, so this is a useful benchmark.
The headline will be the agent count. I would focus on conversion instead. Of roughly 1,700 use cases in the funnel, about a third are in production. That rate separates scaled AI from pilot theatre, and most boards are not yet asking for it.
The disclosure leaves questions open: how many use cases face customers, and how are thousands of personal agents governed? That gap is industry-wide. IBM recently cited a Gartner estimate of more than 150,000 agents per Fortune 500 company by 2028, with only 13% of leaders confident they can govern them.
My view is that enterprises should report AI as a funnel: ideas, in development, in production, and value realised, with a named owner and governance status for each production item. Agent counts measure enthusiasm; conversion measures transformation.
Takeaway: ask for the production count and the conversion rate, not the agent count.
Takeaway: Ask for the production count and conversion rate, not the agent count.

Newsletter post 5: Logistics AI has a long-tail problem: most SMEs are still at the start
LOGISTICS / ASIA
Full post | 182 words | Thought-leadership voice
Hong Kong's LogTech Expo 2026 brought together more than 50 companies showing AI, smart warehousing, robotics, IoT tracking and automated storage, with forums on digital twins and the low-altitude economy. Yet the most important line came from the panels: around 65% of SMEs are still in the early stages of technology adoption.
That gap between showcase and shop floor is familiar across Asia. Large operators run automated terminals and AI routing; many hauliers and warehouse partners still run on spreadsheets and phone calls. In a supply chain, the slowest digital link sets the pace. A PwC survey reported earlier this year found 92% of operations and supply chain executives felt technology investments had not fully met expectations, with integration complexity the most cited reason.
Singapore has noticed. Enterprise Singapore's Catalytic AI Solutions track starts with manufacturing, trade and logistics, packaging solutions rather than leaving each SME to build its own.
My view: logistics leaders should treat partner digitalisation as part of their own transformation scope, with common data standards, shared tools and simple onboarding.
Takeaway: digitise the ecosystem, not just the enterprise.
Takeaway: Digitise the ecosystem, not just the enterprise; the weakest partner sets the pace.
