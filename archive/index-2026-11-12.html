Idea 1: Equinix SG6 shows Singapore will add AI capacity only when it is efficient by design
Hook and angle | News dated 6 Nov 2026 (also reported 9 Nov)
THE HOOK
In Singapore, data-centre capacity is allocated, not just built. Efficiency is the entry ticket.
WHAT HAPPENED (PARAPHRASED)
Equinix opened SG6, its sixth Singapore data centre and the first it has built under the government's Data Centre Call for Application (DC-CFA). The initial investment is US$290 million, with about 1,550 cabinets in phase 1 and up to 20MW of IT capacity at full build-out. It is designed for a PUE of 1.20 and targets Green Mark Platinum, using rooftop and façade solar, NEWater and rainwater harvesting. Customer servers must meet the SS 715:2025 energy-efficiency standard, and liquid cooling supports high-density GPU racks. A co-innovation facility with the NUS Energy Solutions Hub is targeted for 2027.
Why it matters: Rationed capacity means efficiency rules now reach the servers enterprises bring in, not only the building.
Source: Equinix newsroom, 6 Nov 2026; TechNode Global, 9 Nov 2026 (paraphrased; figures company-reported).

Newsletter post 1: Equinix SG6 shows Singapore will add AI capacity only when it is efficient by design
Full post | 185 words | Thought-leadership voice
Singapore does not simply let data centres be built; it allocates the right to build them. Equinix's SG6, opened on 6 November, is the first Equinix facility built under the government's Data Centre Call for Application. The numbers are modest by hyperscale standards, an initial US$290 million and up to 20MW of IT capacity, but the conditions are telling: a design PUE of 1.20, a Green Mark Platinum target, solar on the roof and façade, NEWater for cooling, and a rule that customer servers meet the SS 715:2025 efficiency standard.
My view is that the last condition is the one enterprise IT teams should notice. Efficiency rules are moving from the building to the rack, so the hardware we choose to colocate is now part of the compliance story. Where capacity is rationed, the workloads that win space will be the ones that use it well.
Practically, refresh plans for Singapore-hosted infrastructure should check server efficiency ratings, liquid-cooling readiness for GPU racks, and whether each workload truly needs local hosting. The NUS co-innovation facility, targeted for 2027, is one to watch rather than plan on.
Takeaway: Add server energy-efficiency ratings and cooling readiness to Singapore colocation and refresh plans now.

Full post | 169 words | Thought-leadership voice
Most enterprise AI conversations start with which cloud model to call. Singapore's Centre for Strategic Infocomm Technologies, the Ministry of Defence's technology agency, has started from the other end. Its partnership with Cohere, announced on 5 November, will run Cohere's models and North agentic platform inside highly secure environments, after a pilot showed they could operate on on-premises systems and fit existing workflows. The first uses are practical: secure knowledge retrieval, analysis and report drafting.
My view is that this is the clearest local signal yet that sovereignty is an architecture decision, not a procurement checkbox. Banks, healthcare providers and critical-infrastructure operators face a milder form of the same constraint, and now have a pattern to follow: bring the model to the data, keep a person able to approve or stop the agent, and prove workflow fit in a pilot first.
The caution is that on-premises AI moves the operating burden back in-house: model updates, GPU sizing and agent monitoring become your job. Cohere's regional growth figures are company-reported.
Takeaway: Classify AI use cases by data sensitivity first, then decide where the model runs.

Newsletter post 3: Microsoft walks away from rented Oracle GPUs: AI capacity contracts age like hardware
Full post | 175 words | Thought-leadership voice
When it was announced in 2023, renting Oracle's AI capacity to power Bing looked like smart sourcing. According to Bloomberg reporting carried by Reuters on 6 November, Microsoft has declined to renew and has been switching that capacity off, because it runs on an older generation of Nvidia chips that no longer fits its needs. Oracle says demand for its AI infrastructure, including earlier-generation GPUs, remains strong. Microsoft did not immediately comment.
My view is that the lesson for enterprises is about contract shape, not vendors. AI hardware ages in roughly the time a typical infrastructure contract runs, so a multi-year commitment to one GPU generation is a bet on standing still. If the largest buyer in the market is reviewing its capacity deals, the rest of us should assume our AI commitments will need an exit.
When negotiating GPU or AI-platform capacity, ask for technology-refresh rights, shorter terms or credits that move across generations, and keep at least one alternative provider qualified. The report relies on unnamed sources, so treat the detail as directional.
Takeaway: Write hardware-generation refresh and exit terms into every AI capacity contract.
