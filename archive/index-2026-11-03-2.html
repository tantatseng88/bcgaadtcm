Newsletter post 1: Singapore is now funding AI transformation plans, not AI pilots
SINGAPORE
This week Enterprise Singapore opened its Champions of AI programme with 24 companies, alongside agentic AI workshops for SMEs and a Catalytic AI Solutions track that began with a S$45 million, three-year push in advanced manufacturing. UOB has already attached a number to its ambition: S$900 million of AI-driven value by 2030, after more than 300 use cases and roughly 32,000 staff trained.
What strikes me is the shift in what is being funded. Earlier waves paid for tools and proofs of concept. This wave pays for long-term transformation plans, workforce skills and sector-wide solution packs. That is the right call, because model access was never the bottleneck. The gap is the distance between a pilot that works and a way of working that changes.
My view is that enterprise leaders should read these programmes as a template, not just a subsidy. Three questions are worth adopting: which workflows carry a measurable value target, who owns adoption rather than just delivery, and how will progress be shown to the board every quarter?
UOB’s own framing, deliberate and trustworthy progress over speed, is a useful counterweight to the pressure to announce. Takeaway: in 2027 the leaders will not be those with the most pilots, but those who can state an AI value target, name its owner and measure it.
Takeaway: Name the value target and the owner before approving the next pilot.

Newsletter post 2: Automated ports shift the failure point from the crane to the dependency chain
SG LOGISTICS / IT
Cisco and PSA Singapore are developing an AI-driven observability and incident management platform for Tuas Port, described as the world’s largest fully automated container terminal. It is a proof of concept: live data from applications, infrastructure and networks is brought into one view, and AI maps how services relate, narrows down affected systems and suggests fixes.
The headline is not the AI. It is the admission that a highly automated operation is, at heart, a software estate. When cranes, yard systems and networks are tightly coupled, an outage rarely starts at the equipment. It starts somewhere in the chain of dependencies behind it. Cisco’s research puts average annual downtime cost at US$300 million per organisation, which is a vendor-cited figure but directionally believable.
My view is that observability belongs in the resilience budget, not the tooling budget. The first step is unglamorous: a living map of which business service depends on which system. Without that, AI has nothing reliable to reason over.
A word of caution: this is a proof of concept, and root-cause suggestions are only as good as the telemetry feeding them. Takeaway: before buying AIOps, agree the service map, the outage metrics you will track and who may act on an AI recommendation.
Takeaway: Map business services to systems first; then let AI reason over them.

Newsletter post 3: Enterprise AI agents are becoming governed software, not chatbots
ENTERPRISE IT / CLOUD
SAP’s latest announcements across Joule Studio, Joule Work and its Business AI Platform read like a checklist for the next phase of enterprise AI: no-code and pro-code agent building, access to more than 70 models, multistep workflow planning, stronger governance, and data fabric and ontology features that give agents business context. In parallel, Cresta now lets teams build customer service agents through their own code repositories and release pipelines, with access controls and cost tracking.
The pattern matters more than either vendor. When model choice is a menu of 70 options, model choice stops being the differentiator. What is left is the context an agent can reach, the controls around it and the discipline used to release it. In other words, agents are becoming software artefacts, and they need the same lifecycle as any other production system.
For enterprise architects, my view is that this should change the conversation from which agent to buy to what the agent estate looks like. That means a registry of agents, a named owner for each, defined data access, cost visibility and a release gate.
These are vendor announcements, and the real test is whether governance features are enforced by default. Takeaway: define your agent registry, ownership model and business-context layer before you choose a platform, not after.
Takeaway: Register, own and release agents like production software.

Newsletter post 4: Trusting AI agents starts with identity, approval and human decision rights
ENTERPRISE IT / CYBER
Two product launches this week point at the same question. 1Password for Agents lets AI agents sign in to websites using a person’s logins without the agent ever seeing the password or one-time code, and the person approves each login. Persona introduced an AI suite whose case review agents gather evidence, check it against policy and recommend a decision, while humans stay in control of consequential choices.
Add the reported move by OpenAI to train its most capable models on an offline copy of the web, after an incident in September, and a theme emerges: containment and approval are becoming product features, not afterthoughts. These details come from secondary roundups and vendor statements, so they deserve caution.
My view is that most enterprises are about to give software agents access to systems before they have decided what a non-human identity is. The practical answer is familiar: least privilege, short-lived credentials, logged actions and an explicit statement of which decisions an agent may take alone, which it may recommend, and which are never delegated.
“Human in the loop” is only meaningful when decision rights are written down. Takeaway: give every agent an identity, a permission set and a decision-rights statement, and review them as you would a new joiner’s access.
Takeaway: Write down what each agent can do alone, recommend, or never decide.

Newsletter post 5: AI-agent ordering is still a rounding error for DoorDash, and that is useful news
GLOBAL / LOGISTICS
DoorDash says third-party AI agents have so far sent only several hundred orders, in a business that reported around three million deliveries a day in 2025. It is building its own text-to-order and chatbot tools, while keeping partnerships with outside agents open.
It is tempting to read this as a verdict on agentic commerce. I read it as a reminder about adoption. The technology to place an order through an agent exists. What lags is everything around it: customer habits, trust in letting software spend money, who owns the customer relationship, and whether the platform’s economics survive an intermediary.
For logistics and supply chain leaders, the lesson transfers. Whether the channel is a customer-facing agent or an internal planning copilot, capability is rarely the constraint. Readiness is. My view is that DoorDash’s approach is sensible: build your own agent channel first, keep partner options open, and instrument everything so that you can see real demand rather than forecasts.
Takeaway: treat external agent channels as options to be measured, not as a reason to restructure the business. Fund the change work behind adoption, and let observed usage, not the headline, decide the next investment.
Takeaway: Measure real adoption before restructuring around a new channel.
