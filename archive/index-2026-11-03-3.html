Post 1: Singapore's AI champions programme funds change management, not just technology
SINGAPORE
Enterprise Singapore announced on 27 October, at the opening of SWITCH 2026, a set of measures to help companies build and scale AI. The flagship, Champions of AI, starts with 24 firms, among them DBS, OCBC, UOB, ST Engineering, PSA International, Singapore Airlines and Singtel. The support covers long-term AI transformation roadmaps, company-wide deployment, change management, and workforce redesign and upskilling. A pilot of agentic AI workshops also had more than 280 small businesses build working prototypes for marketing, sales and customer service.
What stands out to me is where the list puts people. Change management and workforce redesign sit inside the programme's definition of support, next to roadmaps and tools. That is a quiet but important correction to the way many AI initiatives are run, where technology is funded first and adoption is hoped for afterwards.
My view is that enterprise AI will be won or lost on adoption, not model choice. Excitement and training alone rarely change daily behaviour; people need workflows redesigned around the tool and a reason to trust it. Building prototypes on real use cases, as the pilot did, is a sound way to create that trust early.
For architects and transformation leads, the practical step is simple: put an adoption plan and a named business owner into every AI business case before the first sprint. Takeaway: budget for the people change at the same moment you budget for the platform.
Takeaway: Budget for the people change at the same moment you budget for the platform.

Post 2: Visa's Senegal MoU shows partnership-led transformation starts with use cases, not platforms
GLOBAL
On 2 November, Visa signed a two-year memorandum of understanding with Senegal's Ministry of Telecommunications and Digital Affairs, its first agreement of this kind with a Senegalese government body. The framework covers digital payments, financial innovation, cybersecurity and regulation, including payments from people to government and from government to people. Both sides will identify use cases together; the report cites no funding, targets or launch dates.
Some will read this as a modest announcement. I read it as an honest picture of how public-sector digital transformation actually starts. Before any platform is built, a government and a private partner must agree which problems are worth solving, which rules apply, and how trust will be protected. Cybersecurity is named in the same breath as payments, not as an afterthought.
My view is that the useful part is the sequencing: shared use-case discovery first, delivery second. Many programmes invert this and sign a vendor contract before the problem is prioritised. An MoU also carries a risk. Without a shortlist, owners and dates, a framework can sit as intent for a long time, and the minister's own framing is about moving from intent to practical solutions.
For anyone running a partnership-led programme, including regional ones touching Singapore, the test is whether the first ninety days produce a ranked use-case list with accountable owners. Takeaway: a partnership agreement is a starting line, and its value is set by how quickly it becomes a prioritised, owned backlog.
Takeaway: Turn the agreement into a prioritised, owned use-case backlog within ninety days.

Post 3: Cloud's fastest growth is not at the biggest provider, so place workloads, not loyalties
ENTERPRISE CLOUD
Synergy Research Group's figures, reported on 30 October, put cloud infrastructure services at US$167.7 billion for the third quarter, up 55 percent year on year, and growth has accelerated for twelve straight quarters. AWS holds 26 percent share, Microsoft Azure 19 percent and Google Cloud 16 percent. Latest-quarter revenue growth was 39 percent at AWS, 48 percent at Azure and 103 percent at Google Cloud, the last partly lifted by sales of its custom chips to other data-centre operators. AI-specific services now make up about a third of the market, and neocloud providers are growing quickly as both partners and competitors.
Look at the pattern: the largest provider is growing slowest, and the market's centre of gravity is shifting toward AI capacity. Share and growth are telling different stories.
My view is that enterprises should resist choosing a cloud by headline rank. The decision that matters is workload placement: which workloads need scarce AI capacity, which need data locality, and which simply need to be cheap and boring. A single-provider default made for the general-purpose estate does not automatically suit AI workloads.
Architects can act now by classifying workloads into those three groups, keeping interfaces and data portable where that is inexpensive, and reviewing placement every year as the market moves. Takeaway: let workload needs, not market share or momentum, decide where each system runs.
Takeaway: Let workload needs, not market share or momentum, decide where each system runs.

Post 4: A 36-hour IT outage is a supply chain event, and logistics should be designed that way
LOGISTICS
A logistics newsletter dated 2 November reports that CSX suffered a near 36-hour IT outage that disrupted billing, terminal gates and railroad interchanges; most core applications were restored, but container backlogs were expected to take several days to clear. The same brief says UPS is expanding customer-applied RFID labelling from 3 million packages a day towards a target of 6 million in 2027. This comes from a single vendor-published roundup, so the details deserve checking against primary sources.
Put the two items together and a pattern emerges. Logistics is being digitised to gain visibility, yet the physical network now depends on the availability of the systems that provide it. A billing or gate system that goes down stops trucks and containers just as surely as a blocked road.
My view is that downtime in logistics should be measured as a supply chain event, not an IT incident. Singapore offers a relevant counterpoint: on 29 October Cisco and PSA Singapore announced a proof of concept for an AI-driven observability and incident-management platform at Tuas Port, aimed at cutting unplanned outages in systems such as the terminal operating system.
For operators, the lesson is to map which physical flows each application gates, set recovery targets by flow rather than by server, and rehearse failure. Takeaway: design logistics IT for graceful degradation, so one outage slows the network instead of stopping it.
Takeaway: Design logistics IT for graceful degradation, so an outage slows the network instead of stopping it.

Post 5: FedEx's one-network plan: a merged network is only as good as its merged ways of working
GLOBAL / LOGISTICS
According to the same 2 November brief, FedEx is merging its Express and Ground operations into a single network under Network 2.0, with a savings target of US$2 billion and completion planned for 2027. It cites quarterly revenue up 12 percent to US$22.9 billion. As with the CSX item, this is a secondary, vendor-published summary, so confirm the figures against FedEx's own filings before quoting them externally.
What interests me is the shape of the programme. This is a transformation led by the operating model, with technology as an enabler: two networks, two sets of processes and two ways of working become one. The prize is stated as a hard number, and the finish line is dated.
My view is that this is the version of transformation that delivers, and the version that is hardest on people. Integration programmes tend to be judged on the savings line while the human side, such as role clarity, local autonomy and trust between legacy teams, is handled informally. Yet that is where benefits leak.
Leaders planning their own consolidations, including those merging regional systems or business units in Asia, can borrow two habits: tie each stage to a measurable outcome, and tell the change story before the org chart, not after it. Takeaway: a merged network is only as good as the merged ways of working beneath it.
Takeaway: Tell the change story before the org chart, and tie each stage to a measurable outcome.
