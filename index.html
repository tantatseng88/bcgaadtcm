Idea 1 · Newsletter post · SINGAPORE · CYBER
Post 1 | Singapore’s quantum-safe grant turns a distant 2035 deadline into a 2026 planning task
Singapore’s Cyber Security Agency has put dates on quantum risk. Organisations exposed to it should have migration plans by 2027 and complete the move to quantum-safe cryptography by 2035, with critical information infrastructure finishing by 2031. The Quantum-Safe Kickstarter Grant, open for pilot projects from 1 November 2026, funds work of up to six months.
The priority areas are revealing: discovering and cataloguing existing cryptography, migrating one system end to end, and tackling operational technology and legacy estates. Most enterprises cannot yet say where cryptography lives across applications, integrations, devices and vendor products. A long deadline also makes deferral easy.
My view: treat quantum-safe migration as an architecture and change programme, not a cryptography upgrade. Start with an inventory mapped to business services, rank systems by how long their data must stay confidential, and use a six-month pilot to learn on one system before committing to a roadmap.
One caution: the 2031 date applies to critical information infrastructure, so first confirm which timeline applies to you. The inventory is needed either way.
Takeaway: Build the cryptographic inventory now; it is the prerequisite for every later decision.

Idea 2 | Singapore leads on AI use, but trust to let AI act is the real adoption gap
THE HOOK
Singapore leads the region in using AI; the open question is how much work people will let it do.
WHAT HAPPENED (PARAPHRASED)
•e-Conomy SEA 2026 (Google, Temasek, Bain) puts generative AI use at about 70% of Singapore’s working population, roughly 3.2 million people; one in three uses it daily.
•Across the region, about 93% of AI users trust AI to some degree as a shopping assistant, but only 3% would let it act on their behalf.
•Consumers mainly use AI to research and compare options, and prefer to make payments themselves.
Why it matters: Usage is easy to count; delegation is what creates value, and it rests on trust.
Source: Fintech News Singapore, 21 Oct 2026 (e-Conomy SEA 2026 report; paraphrased).

Idea 2 · Newsletter post · SINGAPORE · DIGITAL ECONOMY
Post 2 | Singapore leads on AI use, but trust to let AI act is the real adoption gap
The e-Conomy SEA 2026 report from Google, Temasek and Bain puts generative AI use at about 70 percent of Singapore’s working population, roughly 3.2 million people, with one in three users relying on it daily. The same report shows a limit: across Southeast Asia, about 93 percent of AI users trust AI to some degree as a shopping assistant, yet only 3 percent would let it act on their behalf.
That gap between using and delegating is the real story. Asking an assistant to compare options is cheap and reversible. Letting it pay, approve or submit is a different decision, and it rests on confidence in controls rather than on model quality. Consumers voice the same hesitation enterprise leaders feel about agentic workflows.
My view: usage figures flatter us. Adoption is not how many people open the tool; it is the share of decisions they are willing to hand over, and that grows only when guardrails, accountability and error recovery are visible. Internal teams should track delegation depth alongside logins.
These are survey-based regional consumer figures, so the enterprise lesson is an analogy, not a measurement. The sequencing still holds: earn trust on low-risk tasks first.
Takeaway: Track delegation, not access: how much work people trust AI to do.

Idea 3 · Newsletter post · OUTSIDE SINGAPORE · PUBLIC SECTOR
Post 3 | Visa’s Senegal agreement shows transformation can start with citizen payments, but an MoU is only the start
Visa has signed a two-year memorandum of understanding with Senegal’s Ministry of Telecommunications and Digital Affairs. It covers digital payments, financial innovation, cybersecurity and regulation, with a main focus on payments between citizens and the state, in both directions. It is Visa’s first agreement of this kind with a Senegalese government body.
Citizen-to-state payments are a smart place to begin. They are frequent, visible and measurable, and they pull identity, cybersecurity and regulation into a single journey. Ordinary people feel the result quickly, which builds the public and political support that larger platform programmes need.
My view: an MoU is a statement of intent, and the test is whether it names a first use case, an owner and a measure within a year. Programmes that open with one visible citizen journey build the case for change faster than those that open with a platform diagram.
The same applies inside enterprises that sign vendor partnerships: keep ownership of the outcome and the data, and let the partner supply capability. The announcement itself gives no delivery dates or targets.
Takeaway: Pick one visible journey, name its owner and measure, then scale.

Idea 4 · Newsletter post · ENTERPRISE IT · CLOUD
Post 4 | Enterprise AI is moving into systems of record, so identity and permissions will decide who wins
In a single week, OpenAI announced ways to link ChatGPT to Oracle applications and to ServiceNow, so employees can look up information and complete approved workflows in finance, procurement and IT from inside the chat. Microsoft is rolling out Autopilot, a cloud-hosted personal agent with its own identity, mailbox and workspace, to early users this month, alongside approval and retention controls for Copilot memory.
The shift is from answering questions to carrying out work in the systems that hold the records. That makes the chat window less important than the plumbing behind it: identity, entitlements, audit trails and clear accountability when an agent acts. The roundup itself concludes that governance and verifiable outputs will separate lasting approaches from the rest.
My view: architects should stop evaluating assistants as products and start treating them as new principals in the identity model. Each agent needs a named owner, scoped permissions, a log and a defined point where a human approves. If the current role model cannot express that, fix it before widening access.
These are vendor announcements and the controls are early; whether they are enforced by default is what to verify in a proof of concept.
Takeaway: Treat every agent as a user with an owner, scoped rights and an audit trail.

Idea 5 · Newsletter post · LOGISTICS · SUPPLY CHAIN
Post 5 | Supply chain autonomy is arriving in software and in warehouses, and humans still own the decisions
Two announcements show different routes to the autonomous supply chain. SAP says Gartner placed it as a Market Shaper in its first Emerging Market Quadrant for Integrated Supply Chain AI Agents. Its path is phased: agents recommend first, then act within guardrails. One customer has three coordinated agents handling over 90 percent of about 100,000 yearly supplier order confirmations without manual touch.
Walmart, meanwhile, has expanded its agreement with Symbotic toward 25 US regional distribution centres, with an initial commitment of roughly US$6 billion over several years. One bet is on software autonomy, the other on physical automation. Software autonomy depends on clean master data and exception rules; physical autonomy depends on capital and standardised flows.
My view: for logistics and manufacturing firms in Singapore, the practical starting point is not full autonomy but the highest-volume, lowest-variance transaction, such as order confirmations or shipment status. Prove accuracy there, define the exceptions humans own, then extend the agent’s authority.
Results are vendor-reported and come from different markets, so treat them as direction, not benchmarks.
Takeaway: Prove one high-volume transaction, define human exceptions, then extend authority.
