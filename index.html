Newsletter post 1: MAS puts agentic AI, digital money and new rules at the centre of Singapore FinTech Festival 2026
Full post | 190 words | Thought-leadership voice
Singapore's FinTech Festival opens on 18 November, and MAS has chosen an unusually direct theme: the coming rewiring of the financial system, with new risks, new rules and new growth. Agentic AI, tokenisation and digital money top the agenda, and the exhibition floor now has dedicated areas for agentic commerce and AI-enabled payments.
My view is that rewiring is the right word, and enterprises outside banking should take note. An AI agent that can start a payment or change a customer record is not a better chatbot; it is a new actor in your process, with its own access rights, failure modes and audit trail. Most organisations still design AI as a tool that helps a person. The next phase needs operating models in which software acts while people supervise, intervene and stay accountable.
For leaders attending this week, I would take three questions rather than a shopping list. Which of our workflows could an agent run end to end? What runtime controls would we need before letting it act? And who owns the outcome when it gets something wrong? The answers will say more about readiness than any demo.
Takeaway: Treat agentic AI as a new actor in your processes: design its permissions, controls and accountability before you design the demo.

Idea 2: The US and eight partners sign a declaration to protect AI supply chains under Pax Silica
Hook and angle | News dated 15 Nov 2026 (FT via Newsmax/Yahoo); 16 Nov 2026 (The Tribune)
THE HOOK
AI is starting to be treated like energy: a strategic supply chain that governments want to map, protect and diversify. That will reach enterprise architecture faster than most expect.
WHAT HAPPENED (PARAPHRASED)
The Financial Times reported, and Newsmax relayed on 15 November, that the US and partners including Japan, Australia, Israel, the UK, South Korea, India, Qatar and the UAE have signed the San Francisco Declaration, part of the US-led Pax Silica initiative. Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission are expected to sign the following week. Participants will jointly list critical AI technologies and supply chains, covering semiconductors, critical minerals and energy infrastructure, that need more protection; strengthen safeguards against theft or unauthorised technology transfer; form working groups on vulnerabilities; and share information on where to diversify. US Under Secretary Jacob Helberg described supply-chain security as a shared responsibility. The Tribune (16 November) notes that Singapore is among the wider Pax Silica signatories; the reports do not say whether it has signed this declaration.
Why it matters: AI infrastructure choices, from chips to cloud regions, are becoming geopolitical choices that enterprises will need to evidence and explain.
Sources: Newsmax via Yahoo News, 15 Nov 2026, citing the Financial Times; The Tribune, 16 Nov 2026, citing ANI (paraphrased).

Newsletter post 2: The US and eight partners sign a declaration to protect AI supply chains under Pax Silica
Full post | 196 words | Thought-leadership voice
AI supply chains now have their own diplomatic framework. According to the Financial Times, the US and eight partners, including Japan, Australia, the UK, India and South Korea, have signed the San Francisco Declaration under the Pax Silica initiative, with Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission expected to follow. Signatories will jointly list critical AI technologies and supply chains, tighten protections against technology transfer and share information on where to diversify.
My view is that this moves AI sourcing from the procurement office to the boardroom. Until now most enterprises chose models, chips and cloud regions on price, performance and features. From here, provenance, jurisdiction and concentration risk will weigh as much, and customers and regulators will increasingly ask firms to show where their AI stack comes from. Singapore, as a Pax Silica participant and a regional hub, sits in the middle of that conversation.
The practical step is unglamorous: build an AI bill of materials. Map which models, accelerators, cloud regions and data centres each critical workload depends on, mark single points of failure, and agree a fallback for each. It is far cheaper to do this before the rules arrive than after.
Takeaway: Map your AI stack's dependencies now: provenance and concentration risk are becoming board-level questions.

Idea 3: AT&T runs nearly 500 agentic AI workflows by redesigning each process before automating it
Hook and angle | News dated 14 Nov 2026 (SiliconANGLE)
THE HOOK
AT&T's lesson on agentic AI is not about models. It is about deleting steps before an agent ever touches the process.
WHAT HAPPENED (PARAPHRASED)
SiliconANGLE reported on 14 November that AT&T has nearly 500 agentic workflows in production and plans to triple that next year, processing about 45 billion AI tokens a day and more than a million transactions a month. Luke Corbin, assistant vice president of digital transformation and AI at AT&T Business, said a Business Agent Foundry formed in early 2025 maps each process and removes unnecessary steps, handoffs and legacy dependencies before agents are applied. Employees can build personal productivity agents, but customer-facing or business-impacting workflows go through security, privacy and compliance review. Every production workflow starts under human oversight, and agents act on their own only once accuracy reaches the mid-to-high 90% range, with monitoring for drift. Early wins include automating mainframe steps in fibre orders. Corbin said production AI investments return about five times within a year.
Why it matters: It shows a repeatable pattern for scaling agents: central process redesign, tiered review and autonomy earned through measured accuracy.
Source: SiliconANGLE, 14 Nov 2026, case study by Paul Gillin (paraphrased). Figures and returns are company-reported.

Newsletter post 3: AT&T runs nearly 500 agentic AI workflows by redesigning each process before automating it
Full post | 195 words | Thought-leadership voice
AT&T now runs nearly 500 agentic AI workflows in production and plans to triple that next year, according to a SiliconANGLE case study published on 14 November. What stands out is not the scale but the method. A central Business Agent Foundry maps each process and strips out unnecessary steps and handoffs before an agent is applied, and every workflow starts under human oversight until accuracy reaches the mid-to-high 90s.
My view is that this is one of the clearest public templates yet for scaling agents responsibly. Most enterprises do the reverse: they bolt an agent onto an existing process and then wonder why the savings are small and the risks are large. AT&T treats autonomy as something an agent earns with evidence, and it separates personal productivity agents from business-impacting ones with a proper review path. Redesign, tiering and measured trust are what turn pilots into a portfolio.
Enterprise architects can borrow the pattern directly. Set an accuracy threshold before autonomy, route customer-facing agents through security and compliance review, and measure value in labour, revenue, cost and customer satisfaction. The return AT&T reports is company-stated, but the discipline behind it is free to copy.
Takeaway: Redesign the process first, then let agents earn autonomy through measured accuracy and tiered review.

Idea 4: Malaysia plans to make data centres disclose power use, GPU imports and operators by law
Hook and angle | News dated 12 Nov 2026 (The Star); re-reported c.15 Nov (Tom's Hardware)
THE HOOK
In Johor and beyond, AI capacity is no longer just a commercial booking. Malaysia wants to know who uses the power, for which chips, and where.
WHAT HAPPENED (PARAPHRASED)
The Star reported on 12 November that Malaysia's Minister of Investment, Trade and Industry, Johari Abdul Ghani, said the government will make data-centre disclosure mandatory by law. Operators would report electricity usage, the number of GPU chips imported, the operators and hyperscalers behind each facility and its location, with monitoring continuing after approval. Operators would also pay for the power capacity they reserve: in his example, a 100 MW booking with 50 MW used would still be billed for 100 MW, to curb reservations left idle for one to three years. He said 55 of 117 approved projects are operating, and that the Digital Ministry is reviewing laws on re-exports of sanctioned GPUs. Tom's Hardware, in a report cited on 15 November, questioned whether power data alone can catch diverted chips. No date for the law was given.
Why it matters: Capacity, chips and compliance are converging in the region's fastest-growing data-centre market, which will shape cost and contract terms for AI workloads hosted there.
Sources: The Star, 12 Nov 2026 (paraphrased); Tom's Hardware report cited 15 Nov 2026 (headline only; article not opened).

Newsletter post 4: Malaysia plans to make data centres disclose power use, GPU imports and operators by law
Full post | 176 words | Thought-leadership voice
Malaysia plans to make data-centre operators disclose their electricity use, how many GPUs they import, who the operators and hyperscalers are, and where each facility sits. Investment, Trade and Industry Minister Johari Abdul Ghani also said operators will pay for the power capacity they reserve, whether or not they use it. Of 117 approved data-centre projects, 55 are operating.
My view is that this ends the era of treating regional AI capacity as a simple commercial booking. Over-reserving power and leaving it idle has been a rational hedge; under these rules it becomes a cost line. Disclosure of chips and tenants also makes where an enterprise runs its AI workloads visible to regulators. For Singapore firms that use Johor capacity as an extension of their own, this is a planning issue, not just a vendor issue.
Three practical moves follow. Ask providers how the new costs will flow into pricing and contract terms. Right-size capacity commitments to demand you can evidence. And add jurisdiction and disclosure exposure to the criteria in your cloud and colocation strategy.
Takeaway: Treat regional AI capacity as a regulated resource: right-size commitments and weigh disclosure and jurisdiction in hosting decisions.

Full post | 188 words | Thought-leadership voice
DHL Supply Chain is moving from robots to orchestration. According to Robotics & Automation News on 15 November, it is piloting adaptive AI robots and has begun rolling out AI orchestration software that links autonomous mobile robots, packaging equipment and warehouse management systems to allocate resources by order volume. The base is large: more than 9,000 robots and over 1.5 billion robotic picks, as DHL reports.
My view is that orchestration is where the next round of warehouse productivity will come from, and it is mainly an integration problem. A fleet of excellent robots that cannot see the order pipeline or the labour plan simply moves the bottleneck. The winners will treat the warehouse management system, the robot controllers and the workforce schedule as one system with shared data. That also changes jobs on the floor: more time supervising exceptions, less time walking.
For logistics leaders in Singapore and the region, the question is not how many robots to buy. Ask whether your systems can tell every robot and every person what matters most in the next hour, and whether your teams are trained to work that way.
Takeaway: Invest in orchestration and integrated data, not just more robots, and prepare people to supervise the flow.
