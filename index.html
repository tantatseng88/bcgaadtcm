Idea 1: Equinix opens SG6, its first Singapore data centre built under the DC-CFA
Hook and angle | News dated 6 Nov 2026
THE HOOK
In Singapore, data centre capacity is now earned, not just built. SG6 shows the price of entry: AI density, a 1.20 PUE target and efficient customer kit.
WHAT HAPPENED (PARAPHRASED)
On 6 November 2026, Equinix opened SG6, its sixth Singapore data centre and the first it has built under the Government's Data Centre – Call for Application (DC-CFA) process. The initial investment is US$290 million. Phase 1 offers about 1,550 cabinets, with up to 20MW of IT capacity at full build-out. The site is designed for high-density, GPU-heavy AI workloads, uses liquid cooling, targets a PUE of 1.20 and Green Mark Platinum, and requires customer servers to meet recognised energy-efficiency standards in line with SS 715:2025. Senior Minister of State Tan Kiat How attended the opening. Equinix also plans a co-innovation facility with the NUS Energy Solutions Hub from 2027.
Why it matters: Scarce, green capacity means AI infrastructure plans in Singapore must now pass an efficiency test, not just a budget test.
Source: Equinix newsroom and investor release, 6 Nov 2026 (company announcement; figures company-reported; paraphrased).

Newsletter post 1: Equinix opens SG6, its first Singapore data centre built under the DC-CFA
Full post | 191 words | Thought-leadership voice
Equinix has opened SG6, its sixth data centre in Singapore and the first it has built under the DC-CFA, the scheme that now allocates new capacity here. The numbers are notable: US$290 million to start, about 1,550 cabinets in phase 1, up to 20MW at full build, liquid cooling and a target PUE of 1.20. The detail I find most telling is that customer servers must meet recognised energy-efficiency standards before they go in.
My view is that this changes how enterprises should plan AI infrastructure in Singapore. Capacity is no longer just a procurement line item; it is a scarce, regulated resource with entry criteria. If your hardware refresh, cooling assumptions or workload placement were planned for yesterday's racks, your AI roadmap may stall at the data hall door.
For CIOs and architects, three moves follow. First, map which workloads truly need to sit in Singapore for latency, data residency or interconnection, and which can run elsewhere in the region. Second, write energy efficiency into hardware standards now, not at contract signature. Third, treat colocation and cloud partners as part of the architecture, with growth and exit options agreed early.
Takeaway: Plan AI infrastructure in Singapore around scarce, efficient capacity: decide placement, set efficiency standards and lock in partner options early.

Idea 3: Hackers switch supermarket freezers to defrost across 115+ European stores
Hook and angle | News dated 2 Nov 2026 (coverage to 3 Nov)
THE HOOK
The latest retail cyberattack in Europe stole no data. It warmed the freezers. Connected equipment run by vendors is now part of the attack surface.
WHAT HAPPENED (PARAPHRASED)
On 2 November 2026, BleepingComputer reported that attackers had remotely changed refrigeration settings at more than 115 stores across Belgium, the Netherlands, Sweden, Luxembourg, Ireland and Estonia; other reports add Finland. Freezers were shut down or switched to defrost, and one Swedish store reported settings of +50°C that staff could not change. Affected chains included Ahold Delhaize, Carrefour, ICA, Coop and SuperValu, and some stores discarded food. A Belgian refrigeration vendor said the same weakness had been used in attacks in France in October, and that fixing it needs physical changes as well as software updates. No attacker has been named.
Why it matters: Operational technology installed and run by third parties can halt operations and destroy stock without touching IT systems.
Source: BleepingComputer, 2 Nov 2026, citing RTL and retailer statements; follow-up reports to 3 Nov (paraphrased).

Newsletter post 3: Hackers switch supermarket freezers to defrost across 115+ European stores
Full post | 183 words | Thought-leadership voice
Overnight in early November, attackers changed the settings on supermarket freezers and cold rooms in more than 115 stores across six or more European countries. Units were shut down or switched to defrost; one store saw settings of +50°C that staff could not override. Food was thrown away and technicians disconnected installations in the early hours. The vendor says the same weakness was used in France a month earlier.
My view is that this is the clearest warning yet that operational technology belongs in the board's cyber risk register, not in the facilities budget. Freezer controllers, building systems, conveyors and port equipment are often installed, connected and maintained by vendors, with remote access the IT team never reviewed. Each one is a door into operations.
The response is not exotic. Build an inventory of every connected controller and who can reach it remotely. Put vendor remote access behind secure, monitored connections with multi-factor authentication. Agree patch and hardware-fix timelines in contracts. And rehearse what stores, warehouses or plants do when equipment is tampered with, because the loss here was physical stock, not data.
Takeaway: Treat vendor-run operational technology as a cyber risk: inventory it, secure remote access, and rehearse the manual fallback.

Keep a retained team that can measure utilisation and delivery milestones rather than relying on vendor reports. And spread critical workloads across more than one provider where the cost of delay is high. Note that the Microsoft details come from unnamed sources.
Takeaway: Buy AI compute with refresh, exit and delivery terms built in; GPU capacity ages faster than the contract.

Newsletter post 5: Zipline raises US$1.5B as Walmart adds 300 drone delivery sites
Full post | 194 words | Thought-leadership voice
Zipline has raised US$1.5 billion, and Walmart is adding 300 Zipline drone delivery sites across the US under a new multi-year deal. Walmart says it has made more than 2 million drone deliveries since 2021, with the first million reached only in May this year. Zipline reports a median delivery time of 19 minutes in Dallas-Fort Worth last month.
My view is that the lesson here is about scaling, not drones. Walmart did not bolt on a gadget; it plugged autonomous delivery into what it already has: stores close to customers, an app that shows the option when an address is in range, and order flows that treat a drone as one more fulfilment channel. That integration is why a five-year pilot can now become a network.
For logistics leaders in Asia, the lesson holds whether the last mile is drones, robots or lockers. Before buying the vehicle, design the integration: order routing, inventory visibility at the launch site, customer notification and exception handling. Pick a use case where speed changes customer value and the operating model is ready. And these figures are company-reported, so ask for independent unit economics before copying the model.
Takeaway: Scale last-mile automation by integrating it into stores, apps and order flows, not by piloting the vehicle in isolation.
